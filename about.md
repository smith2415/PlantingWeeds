Throughout my career, spanning years in Army Psychological Operations and amateur academia, I've explored mis- and disinformation: how it's made, how it travels, and most importantly, why it works. I've found, analyzed, and offered solutions to propaganda and information attacks from terrorist organizations, nation state actors and unwitting (or witting) third parties across the globe. I've created training for military professionals and civilian practitioners alike, building the skills people need to operate in polluted information environments. Join me as I continue on this journey creating the tools and resources we need to not only identify when we're being deceived, but what to do about it.

What follows is the short version of my approach. The long version is in the two papers on the [Home page]({{ '/#work' | relative_url }}).

## Three words that are not the same
{: #definitions}

Misinformation is simply incorrect information. Malinformation presents largely accurate information in a misleading way. Disinformation is a conscious attempt by an actor to spread misleading or false information to further their goal. An easy way to remember: Misinformation misses the mark, Malinformation is used maliciously, and Disinformation is disingenuous.

That last part matters most. Disinformation is not something that just happens or appears in our feeds. A campaign is always geared towards an objective: a change in the behavior of a target audience that benefits whoever started it.

## Targeted, convenient, emotional
{: #characteristics}

In my experience, disinformation is defined by three characteristics.

**It is targeted.** Every narrative, slogan and hashtag is aimed at a specific audience, chosen because that audience can deliver the goal and is already leaning that way. It is far easier to nudge people towards something in line with their current behavior than to turn them around.

**It is convenient.** The audience has to like the message and believe it supports their own views. This is confirmation bias at work, helped along by anchoring bias: whoever invents a story is, by definition, the first to break it.

**It is emotional.** An emotional message spurs people to act and shortcuts critical thinking. By emotional I don't just mean happy or sad. I also mean righteous, correct and superior.

The propaganda devices catalogued by the Institute for Propaganda Analysis before the Second World War, such as name calling, glittering generalities, card stacking, transfer, testimonial, plain folk and band wagon, are dated and a little simplistic. They still describe the scaffolding much disinformation is built on.

## Why it spreads
{: #spread}

None of us can see everything or be an expert in every field, so we form most of our beliefs from what other people tell us. We get our false beliefs from the same places we get our true ones.

Disinformation hijacks that natural flow. We accept information more readily from people we know and trust, we hear repeated statements as truer, and once a belief becomes a badge of group membership, holding it can matter more than whether it is correct. This is why disinformation is so dangerous in a partisan landscape. A false story adopted widely enough as a political fact may never stop haunting a country.

## The life cycle of a campaign
{: #life-cycle}

Campaigns follow broad, observable phases.

1. **Injection.** The story enters the information environment by illegitimate means, such as a new account, a bot, or a proxy posing as a whistleblower. The point is to create something other people can cite.
2. **Adoption.** Genuine members of the target audience pick it up. The best case for the campaign is a key communicator, a trusted figure whose sharing launders the story and hides where it came from. This is where most campaigns fail.
3. **Influence.** The story becomes the group's preferred talking point and starts to shape what people actually do.

Researcher Ryan McBeth describes the same arc as the MAGIC kill chain: make the goal, acquire the target, generate the disinformation, infiltrate the target, cascade the amplification.

There is good news in this. Because disinformation travels like any other information, it is trackable and traceable. Key phrases are repeated word for word along the way, and those repeated words lead back to the source.

## A tale of two yachts
{: #yachts}

In *Planting Weeds* I walk through a verified Russian campaign from the point of view of the people who built it: a fabricated story that Ukraine's president had bought two mega yachts with embezzled military aid.

The goal was to reduce US military aid to Ukraine. The audience was a group already opposed to that aid on fiscal grounds. The message offered them a symbol of corruption at a time of economic hardship. It started on a YouTube account with 132 subscribers, was picked up by two near-identical proxy news sites, then by large social media accounts, and finally reached the floor of the US House of Representatives. By then a reader was four steps removed from the original author. The yachts were still for sale.

The same case shows the limits of disinformation. Some of the people it reached repeated it, some borrowed its imagery, and some rejected it outright.

## Old stories, new wars
{: #history}

Propaganda also borrows from the past. In *The Final Crusade* I studied how ISIS used a romanticized history of the Crusades to claim historical validity for its own war and ideology. Labelling every opponent a "Crusader" created an all-purpose enemy, justified violence, and painted the world in black and white terms.

The technique is not unique to ISIS. Claiming to be the successor of something already seen as legitimate is a shortcut to legitimacy, and creating an enemy to fight is a powerful way to build a following.

## Pulling weeds
{: #pulling-weeds}

Confronting disinformation is uncomfortable. Going against the grain is hard even when we are certain, and realizing that someone we trust is deceiving us can shake a person to their core. Those discomforts have to be faced anyway.

What works is education: media literacy, critical thinking, and pre-bunking, where proof of disinformation attempts and known actors is published before a campaign can strike.

A simple tool anyone can use is SCAME, a longstanding US Army method for analyzing a piece of information.

- **Source.** Does the author have a noted bias or conflict of interest?
- **Content.** Do the facts and wording seem too good to be true, emotional, or designed to guide me to a conclusion?
- **Audience.** Does this seem to cater to a specific community or type of person?
- **Media.** Am I only seeing this on certain platforms, used mainly by certain audiences?
- **Effect.** Is there an obvious reaction to it, or are people using it in a certain way?

Most of what we come across is not disinformation, but most of it is still trying to influence us. Give every source the same standard of scrutiny, even the sources you like and trust. Notice when emotion is playing a part in a decision, and take a step back.

We will never agree on everything, nor should we. But our leaders need to take a stand against false narratives regardless of who those narratives support, and the rest of us need to keep our political discourse healthy, objective, and not defined by one side versus another.
